What to check before the demo

An AI agent is not a chatbot with a longer prompt. Once it can call a tool, send a message or hit a paid API, one bad prompt or one runaway loop can leak data or burn a budget. I mapped that class of failure to OWASP LLM10, Unbounded Consumption, when I built BleedStop. The demo that looks best in a call is often the one that has not been asked what happens when the model is wrong.

If I were hiring an AI agent developer from Pakistan, or from anywhere else, I would want four answers in plain language. How do you stop prompt injection from reaching a tool or a spend? Where is the cost limit enforced, and what happens when it is hit? How do you evaluate the agent, and what did the eval miss? Can we actually work in the same hours? I am in Pakistan Time, PKT, which is UTC+5 and does not use daylight saving.

A good answer names a mechanism and a miss. "We use a strong model" is not a mechanism. "The prompt says not to book without confirmation" is not a mechanism either, because the model can ignore it. The rest of this note is what those four checks look like on work I can point at, and how I would run the same checks on someone else.

Agent Receptionist, 25 Nov 2026

The Agent Receptionist platform case study is a voice and chat receptionist for many businesses at once. A new business is one JSON file. It ships with 22 sample tenants: 11 hotels and 11 dental clinics. Twenty are fictional, with names and phone numbers in the 555-01xx and Ofcom drama ranges. Two, Erth Abu Dhabi and MM Dental Care, were built from public facts on erth.ae and mmdentalcare.co.uk. Where a fact is not published, including Erth room rates and the MM Dental Care check-up price, the field is null and the agent says the team will confirm it. Nothing is invented to fill the gap.

Hours, prices, policies, bookings and hand-offs are answered by tools and templates. The language model is called only for open FAQ questions, grounded on that tenant's own knowledge passages. On the 25 Nov 2026 platform eval, that was 60 of 670 turns. book_room and book_appointment run only with the confirmation id issued when the caller says yes to the read-back. A model reply is not enough to create the booking.

On 25 Nov 2026, the platform release gate, run in-process with the mock responder, passed 270 of 270 cases and 670 of 670 turns. Hotel was 140 of 140 and dental was 130 of 130. The 15 categories include booking, booking change, side question, hand-off, injection, exfiltration, cross-tenant, tool error and emergency. There were 0 safety failures and 0 writes without confirmation. The gate requires at least 95 percent overall and 100 percent on the safety categories.

On 25 Nov 2026, from 01:25 to 01:30 AM PKT, the same 270 cases and 670 turns passed against Groq's free-plan model openai/gpt-oss-20b, on a cloud box, text only. That run had 0 safety failures, 1 guard block that caught a cross-tenant leak, 60 model turns, 17,681 tokens (about 295 per answer), an FAQ-agent p50 of 0.23 ms and p95 of 401 ms, and a cost of $0. The same day, npm test reported 147 passing tests and 1 skipped. The skipped test is the Postgres row-level security test, which needs PG_TEST_URL. With that variable set on a local Postgres 17, the suite was 153 of 153. The dental eval gate the same day, on the mock responder, was 30 of 30.

What that run does not show matters as much as the score. Speech recognition, speech synthesis, real time-to-first-audio, phone-line latency and speech quality were not measured. The latencies on that page are in-process, and the FAQ p95 includes the Groq call. The Docker image has not been built yet, because Docker was not available on the build box. The source is public at usmamalik345/agent-receptionist.

There is an earlier, separate write-up, the Agent Receptionist case study, on fictional hotel and dental businesses. Do not mix its numbers with the 25 Nov gate. On that earlier study the deterministic eval was 44/44 (hotel 23/23, dental 21/21), the live Groq eval was 43/44, and a live 20-question test was 19/20. The miss was an eval regex that was too narrow; the reply itself was right. Policy-routed replies use 0 tokens. Merged fixes in Pipecat, Genkit and Strands

A candidate who has only wrapped a framework has not shown they can debug one. My public record is on the open source page, and each merged pull request has its own write-up.

Pipecat #5760 was merged on 15 Sep 2026. An intermediate function call result, marked not final, was cancelling both the per-tool timeout and the global function-call timeout. A tool that reported progress disarmed its own deadline. The fix leaves the deadline armed until the result is final. A progress update does not clear it and does not restart it. The shorter note is the Pipecat #5760 blog post.

Genkit #6346 was merged on 23 Sep 2026, in the Python OpenAI plugin. The embedder forwarded encodingFormat: 'base64', which turned off the SDK's own decoding, and embedding validation failed. The embedder no longer forwards base64. float is still forwarded, because that is a different request. The pull request's repro used a mock transport, not a live provider.

Strands Agents harness SDK #4313 was merged on 14 Sep 2026. AgentResult.to_dict() promised a dictionary that JSON can serialize, but a Bedrock message can contain raw bytes, and json.dumps then raised TypeError. The fix passes the message through the same byte encoder session persistence already uses, and the reverse path restores the original bytes.

LiveKit Agents #7186 is a different timeout, on EndCallTool. It was still open when I checked the pull request on 17 Nov 2026. Until it is merged, that is a proposal, not a change in LiveKit Agents. I would want a candidate to say that out loud.

BleedStop, measured on one machine

BleedStop is a control plane in front of LLM, SMS and payment APIs. An app calls POST /decide before it spends, and gets back ALLOW, LIMIT, CHALLENGE or DENY from deterministic per-tenant rules. Redis holds the budget so several instances share one ceiling. The BleedStop build post walks through that design. The cost implications are in how much an AI agent costs to build and run.

The measured numbers below were all taken by me on one shared 8 vCPU Linux machine. The load generator and the services share that hardware, so I treat the absolute rates as a lower bound for a real cluster. I do not have a production customer figure for money saved. The public Vercel demo is not the cluster that produced those numbers. The raw results are in usmamalik345/bleedstop.

The security check sits in front of the model. The default is a phrase list. A DeBERTa classifier is a second stage I can turn on. On the held out set of 80, phrase-list recall was 22.5% at a 0% false positive rate, and classifier recall was 77.5% at a 5% false positive rate. Two-stage recall on that set was 80.0% at the same 5.0% false positive rate. The 96.4% phrase-list figure is the dev set the list was tuned on, not a held out score. I wrote that distinction up in the prompt injection defence post. The golden set and the rules share an author, so the 48.0% recall is not a field result. I would want a candidate to say the weak number too.

Every BleedStop decision is written to a Postgres audit table. A trigger blocks UPDATE and DELETE, so a decision can be reviewed later and cannot be edited in place. Fail open and fail closed are different products: if the classifier sidecar is down, the default is to keep the phrase-list verdict, and a closed mode turns that outage into a block. Ask which one you are buying.

How to evaluate a candidate

Ask for the eval suite, the date it was run, and the case it missed. A score without the miss is a marketing slide. On Agent Receptionist the miss I will name is not a failed booking: the 25 Nov Groq run caught one cross-tenant leak in the guard, and the earlier private study missed one check because the eval regex was narrower than the correct reply. On BleedStop the miss is recall. Precision of 100% on 12,140 labeled requests still leaves 48.0% recall, which means the rules miss attacks they were not written to see.

Ask whether the guardrail runs before the model, in code, or whether the prompt says to be careful. A sentence in a prompt is not a guardrail. In Agent Receptionist the injection screen is rules, and it runs before any tool. Booking writes wait for a confirmation id, not for the model to sound sure. In BleedStop the budget decision is POST /decide, not a line in the system prompt.

Ask where the cost ceiling lives. A limit written in a prompt is not a limit. A limit stored in one process splits as soon as you run a second instance behind a load balancer. BleedStop keeps the ceiling in Redis so three instances share it. The $5 figure in the test is a correctness ceiling I chose for that run. It is not a recommended budget, and it is not money saved for a customer.

Ask for a repository you can open: a merged pull request, a public eval, or a case study that names what was not measured. A private demo with no dated result is not proof. The three merged fixes above are in Pipecat, Genkit and Strands, and the Agent Receptionist platform repository is public. The earlier receptionist study is private on purpose, and I say so. The POS and the doctor booking platform are company products. If someone cannot show either a public change or a dated measurement, you are hiring the demo.

Ask which hours are actually shared. PKT is UTC+5 and does not use daylight saving, so the overlap changes when your country starts or ends daylight saving, not when mine does. A recruiter who says "full overlap with the US" without naming East Coast or Pacific, and without saying evening, is skipping the only fact that affects the standup.

Gulf mornings fall inside my working day. PKT is one hour ahead of Dubai and two hours ahead of Saudi Arabia and Qatar, so most of a Gulf working day overlaps. UK mornings fall inside my working day: 9:00 in London is early afternoon for me in both summer and winter. Central Europe is the same shape. A 9:00 start in Berlin, Amsterdam, Paris or Zurich is 12:00 PKT in summer and 13:00 PKT in winter, so standups and reviews fit. A 17:00 close in Central Europe is 20:00 or 21:00 for me, which is late for a daily meeting, so I would keep the late European afternoon in writing.

A US East Coast morning is evening for me, 18:00 or 19:00 PKT. US Pacific mornings are later still: 9:00 PDT is 21:00 PKT, and 9:00 PST is 22:00 PKT. I can do that on purpose. I would not pretend it is a shared morning.

Australia is the overlap that is easiest to get backwards. A 9:00 Sydney morning is 4:00 PKT in winter and 3:00 PKT in summer, which is too early for a daily standup. The useful window is your afternoon and my morning: 9:00 PKT is 14:00 AEST or 15:00 AEDT in Sydney. Perth is closer. 9:00 AWST is 6:00 PKT, and 9:00 PKT is 12:00 AWST, so the shared window is still a Perth late morning and my morning, not a 9:00 Perth standup. Queensland stays on AEST all year. Those city rules are written out on the Australia hire page.
